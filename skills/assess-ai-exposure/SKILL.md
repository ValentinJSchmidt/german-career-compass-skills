# Assess AI Exposure

Answer the question behind "will AI take my job?" in a form someone can act on: which tasks
in this specific role are under pressure, which are not, what actually slows or speeds that
down in this industry, and what is worth building over the next few years.

This is an **evidence layer**, not an oracle. It reports what is visible today with dated
sources and is explicit about what is unknown.

## Prerequisites

Requires the occupation described **as tasks** — what the day is actually made of — not just
a title. "Sachbearbeiter" and "Lagerist" cover enormously different work. Get the task list
from the profile, or ask for it first.

Requires live web/search access for current evidence. If unavailable, say so, and give only
the task-level structural reasoning without citing figures.

## Workflow

1. Decompose the role into 5-10 concrete tasks with a rough share of the working day. Use
   the person's own description first, then an official occupation profile to catch tasks
   they did not mention.
2. Read `references/ai-exposure-rubric.md` and `references/durable-signal.md`.
3. For each task, classify: **under substitution pressure**, **being augmented**, or **not
   meaningfully affected** — on evidence, not vibes.
4. Identify the **bottlenecks** that actually govern deployment in this industry and firm
   size: cost, liability, regulation, physical variability, integration with existing
   systems, capital cycles, works councils, and whether the wage bill is even large enough
   to justify the investment. Bottlenecks are usually why a technically automatable task
   stays human for years.
5. Search for current, dated evidence: sector reports, employer statements, chamber and
   association publications, labour-market analyses, occupation profiles. Prefer German and
   sector-specific sources over general commentary.
6. Assemble the picture for a **two-to-five-year horizon**. Do not forecast beyond it.
7. Turn it into a build list: the two or three concrete things worth learning, grounded in
   the tasks that are growing rather than in generic "AI skills".

## Output

- **The role in tasks** — the decomposition, with rough time shares
- **Under pressure** — which tasks, why, with dated evidence
- **Being augmented** — tasks that get faster or change shape rather than disappear, and
  what that does to the skill mix
- **Holding** — tasks that look durable in this industry, and *what specifically* makes them
  so (not "human touch" — a named bottleneck)
- **What governs the pace here** — the real constraints on deployment in this sector and at
  this firm size
- **What this means over 2-5 years** — the honest shape: usually "the job changes", less
  often "the job shrinks", rarely "the job goes"
- **What to build** — two or three specific things, tied to the growing tasks
- **What I don't know** — stated plainly, including where the evidence is thin or the
  sources disagree

## Rules

- **Never say "your job is safe" or "your job will be replaced."** Both are unfounded and
  neither helps anyone decide anything.
- **Never sort occupations into safe and doomed, and never sort by collar.** Blue-collar work
  is not uniformly protected — warehouse picking, routine inspection, and standard logistics
  are among the most automation-adjacent work there is. White-collar work is not uniformly
  exposed — regulated, accountable, and relationship-carried office work is sticky. Assess
  the tasks, not the category.
- **Treat genuinely uncertain cases as uncertain.** Nobody credibly knows what software
  engineering looks like in five years: the work is text-heavy, which speeds substitution,
  and integration-, accountability-, and judgment-heavy, which slows it — and demand effects
  could go either way. Say that, rather than picking the confident-sounding half.
- **Stay inside the plannable band.** Scenarios where AI takes over most work are out of
  scope for advice — not because they are impossible, but because no career plan survives
  them, so they cannot inform a decision. If someone raises it, say exactly that: it is a
  real worry and an unplannable one, and the useful work is in the range where choices still
  change outcomes. Do not dismiss the concern, and do not plan around it.
- Never invent percentages, automation scores, timelines, or job-loss figures. Cite a dated
  source or say you did not find one.
- Do not treat one consultancy projection as fact. Where sources disagree, say so.
- Mark evidence older than two years as stale — this field moves.
- Exposure is one input into a decision, alongside pay, hours, commute, health, and what the
  person actually wants. Never let it override the rest of the profile.
- Where exposure is real, always pair it with a route. Exposure without a next step is just
  fear, and fear is not advice.
