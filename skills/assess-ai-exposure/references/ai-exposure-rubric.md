# AI Exposure Rubric

## Start from tasks, never from the job title

A job is a bundle of tasks. Automation acts on tasks; employment acts on bundles. Almost all
useless AI career advice comes from skipping this step and rating a title.

Decompose into 5-10 tasks with a rough share of the day. Ask the person, then check an
official occupation profile for tasks they did not mention — people under-report the
coordination, checking, and problem-fixing parts of their own job, which are often the parts
that hold.

## Rate each task

**Under substitution pressure** — a deployed system can already do most of this task at
acceptable quality and cost *in this industry*. Not "a demo exists". Not "a model can do it
in principle".

**Being augmented** — the task gets faster or easier, but a person still drives it. The
common outcome. Watch what it does to the skill mix: augmentation often raises the floor
(more judgment, more checking, more exception handling) rather than removing the role.

**Not meaningfully affected** — physically, legally, or economically out of reach for now.
Name *which* of those three it is.

## Then ask what actually governs the pace

A technically automatable task can stay human for a decade. What decides it:

- **Cost and wage bill** — if a firm employs four people at this task, nobody is buying a
  system for it. Small-firm work is often protected by simple economics.
- **Liability** — who is legally on the hook when it goes wrong, and can that attach to a
  system? Sign-off, inspection, and care decisions move slowly for this reason.
- **Regulation and licensing** — where the law names a qualified person, the task does not
  move until the law does.
- **Physical variability** — a structured warehouse aisle and an unstructured building site
  are entirely different problems.
- **Integration** — most workplaces run on messy legacy systems and undocumented process.
  Last-mile integration is where a lot of automation quietly stalls.
- **Capital cycles** — machines get replaced on ten-year cycles, not on release schedules.
- **Works councils and negotiation** — in Germany this genuinely changes the pace, and it is
  legitimate to say so.

**Bottlenecks are usually the most useful part of this analysis for the person**, because
they explain *why* something will or will not change where they actually work.

## Source bias — the biggest practical risk in this skill

Searching automation topics does not return neutral evidence. It returns, in roughly this
order of volume:

- **Vendor material** — system makers and integrators, whose business is selling the
  automation. They describe capability, not deployment, and rarely mention cost, payback
  period, or the firms that looked and declined.
- **Trade press** — logistics, industry, and technology magazines that run on vendor
  advertising and on novelty. A pilot at one large distribution centre becomes "the
  warehouse of the future".
- **Content marketing by training providers** — blogs with titles like "get out of the
  warehouse before it's too late", written to sell a retraining course. The fear framing
  *is* the sales pitch.
- **Consultancy projections** — headline percentages, widely quoted, rarely re-derived.

Left unchecked, this pipeline produces exactly the doom framing this skill exists to
prevent, while looking like research.

Rules:

- **Separate capability from deployment.** "A robot can pick 600 items an hour" is a
  capability claim. The question is whether firms of *this size* in *this region* have
  bought it, and what stopped the ones that did not.
- A demo, a pilot, a trade-fair announcement, and a press release are **not** evidence of
  deployment. Say which one you actually found.
- Weight in this order: labour-market analysis and official occupation data → employer and
  chamber statements → sector associations → trade press → vendor material.
- Treat a provider's warning about an occupation as marketing for their course, never as
  evidence about the occupation.
- Where the only sources found are vendors and trade press, **say so** and mark the exposure
  assessment as weakly evidenced. That is an honest and useful answer.
- Large-employer coverage crowds out small-employer reality. Most people do not work in an
  automated distribution centre, and the wage-bill bottleneck applies hardest at the small
  end. Ask what firm size the evidence is actually about.

## Horizon

Assess **two to five years**. Beyond that, say plainly that it is not forecastable and stop.

Do not extrapolate a curve. Do not repeat a consultancy's headline number as fact. Where
sources disagree, report the disagreement — that is information, not a failure to conclude.

## The plannable band

Exclude scenarios where AI takes over most work across the economy. Not because they are
impossible — because **no career plan survives them**, so they cannot discriminate between
options. Advice is only useful across the range where different choices lead to different
outcomes.

If the person raises it, say that directly and without condescension: it is a real worry, it
is not a plannable one, and in that world the plan was never the thing that mattered. Then
return to the range where their choices do change something. Do not dismiss the concern, and
do not build a plan around a scenario that makes planning meaningless.

## What to say, and what never to say

Say: which tasks are moving, which are holding, what governs the pace here, what that
probably means over the next few years, and what is worth building.

Never say:

- "Your job is safe." Not knowable.
- "AI will replace this job." Not knowable, and it disables the person rather than informing
  them.
- "Blue-collar work is protected." False as a general claim.
- "Anything at a desk is finished." False as a general claim.
- Any percentage, score, or date you did not source.

## Turning it into a build list

Two or three concrete things, tied to the tasks that are growing in this specific role. Not
"learn AI".

Good build items look like: the checking and exception-handling part of a task that is being
augmented; the licence or sign-off authority that makes someone the person who can approve;
the systems knowledge that makes someone the one who integrates the new tool; the customer
or team-facing part that carries trust; the supervisory step above the current role.
