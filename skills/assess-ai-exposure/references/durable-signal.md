# What Slows And Speeds Substitution

Tendencies, **not** a safe list. Every item here is a pressure, not a verdict, and any of
them can be overturned by a change in cost, capability, or law. Use them to reason about a
specific task in a specific industry — never to sort occupations into safe and doomed.

## What tends to speed substitution up

- The work is mostly **text, code, images, or structured data** — no physical world involved
- The output is **standardized** and looks the same every time
- The work is already **done remotely**, so it is already digitized end to end
- Output is **easy to check** — errors are cheap and visible
- **Low liability** — nobody is legally answerable if it goes wrong
- A **high wage bill** on that one task, so there is a real budget to attack it
- **Abundant training data** for exactly this task
- Little integration needed with existing systems

## What tends to slow it down

- **Physical variability** — unstructured, changing environments, unfamiliar objects
- **On-site presence** required, especially at other people's premises
- **Licensing or regulated sign-off** — where the law names a qualified person
- **Liability that must attach to someone** who can be held responsible
- **Trust and relationships** carried by a specific person over time
- **Last-mile integration** into messy legacy systems and undocumented processes
- **Capital cost** of the hardware, and long replacement cycles
- **A small wage bill** — nobody automates a task done by three people in a firm of twelve
- **Negotiation friction** — works councils, collective agreements, professional bodies

## The symmetry rule

Do not turn these lists into "trades safe, office exposed". Both halves are wrong.

**Blue-collar work is not uniformly protected.** Warehouse picking and sorting, standard
long-haul logistics, routine visual inspection, and repetitive line production are among the
most automation-adjacent work in the economy — structured environments, high volume, and a
wage bill big enough to justify capital. Someone in a modern distribution centre may face
more concrete near-term change than someone doing regulated administrative work.

**White-collar work is not uniformly exposed.** Where the work carries accountability, a
licence, a signature, a long-standing client relationship, or knowledge of how one specific
messy organization actually runs, it is sticky — and often stickier than the general
commentary suggests.

**Trades vary enormously among themselves.** Work in unpredictable environments — repair,
retrofit, installation in old buildings, fault-finding, anything on a customer's premises —
sits very differently from repetitive fabrication in a controlled workshop.

## The honest uncertain cases

Some occupations sit on both lists at once, and the right answer is to say so.

**Software engineering** is the clearest example. It is text-heavy, well-represented in
training data, checkable, and often remote — all accelerants. It is also integration-heavy,
accountability-heavy, and dominated by figuring out what should be built rather than typing
it — all brakes. Demand effects could go either way: cheaper software could mean fewer
developers, or much more software and more of them. **Nobody credibly knows what this looks
like in five years.** Say that, and help the person plan for a role that changes shape
rather than for a specific prediction.

Others in the same position: bookkeeping and accounting, translation, paralegal work,
customer support, radiology reporting, graphic design, and technical drawing. In each, name
the accelerants, name the brakes, and refuse to resolve it into a single confident sentence.

## Using this in advice

- Name the specific mechanism, never "the human touch". "A person has to be liable for the
  sign-off" is useful. "People will always want a human" is not.
- Explain the bottleneck that applies **where this person actually works**. Firm size and
  industry change the answer more than the occupation does.
- A slowing factor is a reason something has time, not a reason it is permanent.
- Never use this file to reassure. Use it to explain.
