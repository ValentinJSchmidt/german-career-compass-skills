# Outreach Rubric

## Length

- **Anschreiben / application:** under one page. Three short paragraphs is plenty.
- **Initiativbewerbung:** shorter still — five or six sentences. It is an opener, not a case.
- **Phone script:** thirty seconds of talking. Anything longer will not survive the call.
- **Internal conversation:** three points, written on one card.

Long is not thorough. Long is unread.

## The phone call

For trades, care, logistics, hospitality, and small firms generally, this is the highest-value
instrument in this whole skill set — those employers frequently never advertise, and a call
reaches a decision-maker in a way a portal never will.

Structure:

1. **Who, and why them specifically.** "Mein Name ist —. Ich habe gesehen, dass Sie
   [verified fact about the firm]." One line.
2. **What they can do**, concretely and with evidence. Not "I'm motivated" — "Ich habe vier
   Jahre im Lager gearbeitet, Staplerschein, und die Schichtübergabe koordiniert."
3. **The ask, made small.** "Suchen Sie jemanden — oder lohnt es sich, dass ich Ihnen meine
   Unterlagen schicke?"

Always include what to say when the answer is no, because it usually is:

> "Kein Problem. Darf ich Ihnen trotzdem meine Unterlagen schicken, falls sich etwas ergibt?"

That question is the entire point of the call. Small firms hire when someone leaves, and the
person whose CV is already in the drawer gets called first. Tell the person this — it turns
a rejection into the expected outcome rather than a failure.

Practical notes worth passing on: call in the morning for trades, avoid Monday mornings and
Friday afternoons, ask for the owner or the Meister by name if discovery found one, and
expect a brusque tone that means nothing.

## Written applications

- **Open with evidence, not with wanting.** "Ich habe vier Jahre im Lager gearbeitet" beats
  "Ich interessiere mich sehr für Ihr Unternehmen."
- **One specific thing about this employer**, drawn only from what was actually verified. If
  discovery found nothing specific, write nothing specific — a generic sentence is better
  than an invented one, and far better than flattery.
- **Answer the advertisement.** If it names three requirements, address those three.
- **Name the gap once, plainly.** "Den Staplerschein habe ich nicht, den kann ich aber
  kurzfristig machen." Then move on. Do not apologize twice.
- **Say when you can start**, and only what is true given the person's constraints.
- **Close with a small ask**, not a grand one: a conversation, a call, a trial day.

## Career changes and gaps

- A career change needs one sentence of reason, not a story. "Nach zehn Jahren im Lager
  möchte ich etwas, das ich auch mit fünfzig noch machen kann" is honest, complete, and
  respected.
- A gap needs one sentence and no apology. Care for a relative, illness, raising children,
  and looking for the right thing are all normal, and most employers have seen all of them.
- Never invent a reason, and never let a draft imply the person was doing something they
  were not.
- Do not editorialize about the person's history in your own commentary either. Draft the
  line, and move on.

## The internal conversation (track a)

Different instrument entirely. The person already has the job; they are asking for a change
in it.

- **Lead with what the employer gets.** "Wir verlieren viel Zeit an —. Wenn ich [Schulung]
  mache, kann ich das übernehmen."
- **Name the specific thing** — a course, a certificate, a responsibility. A vague "I'd like
  to develop" gets a vague answer.
- **Ask about the employer paying for it.** Many do, especially where they struggle to fill
  the role. This is the cheapest funding route in the whole set and the most overlooked.
- **Propose a next step with a date**, not an open wish.
- Have the person write the three points on a card before the conversation. They will need it.

## Tone

Follow `../../career-compass/references/advising-stance.md`. Concretely:

- Plain language. Match how the person actually speaks — a draft in a register they would
  never use collapses the moment they get to the interview.
- No superlatives about the employer and no self-marketing vocabulary.
- Confident, not pleading. The person is offering work, not asking a favour.
- Never write anything the person would have to bluff their way through if asked about it.
